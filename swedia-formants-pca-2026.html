One important clarification: “Bark-filtered spectra” means measuring spectral energy through a bank of auditory-frequency filters. It does not normally mean applying one broad band-pass filter to the waveform.

## What the cited work did

Jacobi, Pols, and Stroop used:

- 20 Bark filters extending to 21 Bark.
- Level normalization of each spectrum to 80 dB.
- Averaging of the first two filters to reduce F0-related variation.
- Midpoint measurements for anchor monophthongs.
- Measurements at 10% and 90% for diphthongs and long vowels.
- Measurements every 10 ms when studying trajectories.
- PCA trained on speaker means for the anchor vowels `/a i u/`.
- Speaker-relative normalization because recording quality shifted and resized PC vowel spaces.

Their first three PCs explained 93% of the anchor-vowel spectral variance; PC1 correlated most strongly with F1 and PC2 with F2. They also found the spectral analysis sensitive to signal-to-noise ratio, despite being less dependent on tracking decisions. [Jacobi et al. paper](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/4394344/47200_isij.pdf)

Leinonen applied essentially this family of methods directly to SweDia:

- Nineteen Swedish vowels.
- Bark-filtered spectra.
- Separate male and female analyses, using approximately 2–17 Bark for men and 3–18 Bark for women.
- Averaging repeated productions per speaker.
- Two rotated components explaining nearly 80% of variance.
- PC1 interpreted mainly as vowel height and PC2 as tongue advancement.
- Geographic maps and aggregate distance analyses for dialect comparison.

That makes this more than merely plausible for the present corpus: it is close to a partial replication using the same underlying SweDia material. [Leinonen thesis record and chapters](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/an-acoustic-analysis-of-vowel-pronunciation-in-swedish-dialects/)

## Recommended first implementation

I would build a reproducible pilot before processing all 288 recordings.

For every selected vowel token:

1. Read its `seg` interval from the TextGrid.
2. Extract spectra at normalized time points, preferably 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 90%.
3. Compute auditory-filter energies across approximately 1–21 Bark.
4. Convert power to dB.
5. Apply explicit level normalization while retaining the original level as a diagnostic.
6. Save the full filter vector for every token and time point.
7. Fit PCA on a clearly defined reference set.
8. Project all remaining tokens using the same fitted PCA.
9. Compare PC scores with existing Bark F1/F2 measurements.

The five normalized time points are preferable to midpoint-only measurements here. They capture diphthongization while remaining compact. A 10 ms trajectory can be added later if the pilot shows useful structure.

## PCA design

There are three defensible models worth comparing:

- Global PCA: fit one model to all speakers and target vowels. Easy to interpret and appropriate for a first exploratory analysis.
- Anchor-vowel PCA: fit loadings using corner/anchor vowels, following Jacobi and Leinonen more closely. This needs careful mapping because the current eight targets do not provide completely ideal `/a i u/` anchors.
- Sex- or speaker-group-specific PCA: closer to Leinonen, but less convenient for direct comparison because scores from independently fitted PC spaces are not automatically commensurable.

I recommend global PCA as the primary pilot, with anchor-trained PCA as a sensitivity analysis. Do not fit a separate PCA for every speaker or village: component axes would then vary and could not be compared directly.

Varimax rotation should be optional. Unrotated PCA is simpler, uniquely variance-ordered, and readily available through NumPy. A rotated two-component result would facilitate a closer replication of Leinonen.

## Major methodological risks

Spectral PCA avoids formant identity errors, but it is not automatically noise-proof.

The important confounds are:

- Microphone and recording-channel differences.
- Background noise and spectral tilt.
- Speaker F0, especially in the lowest auditory bands.
- Overall signal level.
- Sex and vocal-tract-length differences.
- Spectral leakage from consonants near vowel boundaries.
- Unequal numbers of repetitions per speaker and vowel.
- PCA learning recording source or speaker identity instead of vowel quality.

Accordingly, every output row should retain recording source, speaker, duration, time point, RMS level, and some spectral/noise diagnostic. We should explicitly test whether PC1/PC2 predict `Media` versus `sounds`; strong source separation would be a warning.

The single 48 kHz recording should be resampled to 16 kHz, or its spectral analysis should be limited to the same maximum frequency as all other recordings.
